# Modernizing India's Cyber Legal Architecture: Digital India Act and AI Governance

The rapid evolution of generative AI, algorithmic bias, deepfakes, dark patterns, and platform monopolies has highlighted the structural inadequacy of the **Information Technology (IT) Act, 2000**. The Government of India is overhauling the cyber regulatory framework through the **Digital India Act (DIA)**, complementing the **Digital Personal Data Protection (DPDP) Act, 2023**.

## Limitations of the Existing IT Act, 2000

1. **Pre-Smartphone Era Design**: Enacted primarily to foster e-commerce and give legal recognition to electronic records. It lacks provisions tailored to modern algorithmic feeds, cloud computing, and social media dynamics.
2. **Blanket Safe Harbor (Section 79)**: Intermediaries have traditionally enjoyed legal immunity for third-party content provided they observe due diligence. In the era of algorithmic curation and monetized amplification, blanket safe harbor is deemed obsolete.
3. **Absence of AI and Emerging Tech Guardrails**: No statutory mechanism to evaluate high-risk artificial intelligence models, synthetic media, or quantum cryptographic vulnerabilities.

## Core Pillars of the Proposed Digital India Act (DIA)

### 1. Open and Safe Internet (Digital Citizen Rights)
- Codifying algorithmic accountability and transparency.
- Outlawing **dark patterns** (manipulative user interfaces that coerce consent).
- Enhanced safeguards against cyberbullying, non-consensual deepfakes, and automated disinformation campaigns.

### 2. Differentiated Regulatory Framework
Instead of treating all online platforms identically, the DIA proposes risk-based classification:
- **E-commerce platforms**
- **Social media intermediaries**
- **Search engines and ad-tech networks**
- **High-risk Artificial Intelligence (AI) systems**

### 3. Re-evaluating "Safe Harbor"
- Intermediaries that actively recommend, curate, algorithmically promote, or modify user-generated content will face stricter accountability rather than enjoying automatic safe harbor immunity.

### 4. Risk-Based Governance of Artificial Intelligence
- Drawing inspiration from global models (*e.g., EU AI Act*), AI applications are categorized by risk:
  - **Unacceptable Risk**: Social scoring, subliminal behavioral manipulation (strictly prohibited).
  - **High Risk**: AI used in biometric identification, critical infrastructure, judicial decisions, credit scoring (requires audits, explainability, human-in-the-loop oversight).
  - **Limited/Minimal Risk**: Chatbots, spam filters (requires clear synthetic media watermarking and transparency labels).

## Strategic Balance for India
As the host of the GPAI summit and home to a burgeoning startup ecosystem, India’s AI governance model seeks to avoid over-regulation that stifles domestic innovation, while ensuring constitutional rights to privacy, dignity, and informational self-determination under **Article 21** (*K.S. Puttaswamy judgment*).
